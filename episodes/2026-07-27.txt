The most important story right now is the sudden, tentative de-escalation between the United States and Iran, and the oil shock it just reversed.

Late last week the US halted its bombing campaign against Iran, after roughly two weeks of nightly strikes. 
A senior Iranian official told Reuters that Tehran will halt its own attacks as long as the United States keeps its bombing pause in place, describing the stance as "attack for attack."
 Washington hasn't resumed strikes since.

The de-escalation is fragile. 
Iran-backed Houthi forces in Yemen claimed responsibility for attacks on facilities associated with Saudi Aramco at the Red Sea ports of Jizan and Yanbu over the weekend.
 And a Reuters source quoted by Yahoo Finance said there's more scepticism than optimism that the halt will hold.

Markets reacted hard. 
Brent crude futures fell six percent to around ninety one dollars a barrel, while US crude dropped about five and a half percent to just over eighty four dollars.
 That's a sharp reversal — 
Brent had briefly hit one hundred and two dollars a barrel last week, thirty dollars above where it started the month.


The timing matters for policy on both sides of the Atlantic. The Federal Reserve meets this week, and 
is expected to leave interest rates unchanged, though rising oil prices have prompted investors to sharply increase bets on a rate hike later this year.
 Adding to the tension: 
Trump is considering resuming major combat operations in Iran, potentially a joint campaign with Israel,
 when Netanyahu visits the White House on Tuesday — 
an alliance that has shown signs of strain in recent months over whether to keep military pressure on Iran or pursue diplomacy.

Separate talks reportedly cover financing for chip purchases that could total another three hundred fifty billion dollars.
 Reuters hasn't independently confirmed the figures, so treat them as reported, not settled.
